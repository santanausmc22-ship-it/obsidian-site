LESSONS WRITTEN IN SCARS

A Different Kind of Strength

Sometimes the strongest thing a father can do is soften.

Tonight, one of my daughters was having a meltdown over something that seemed small.

Small to me, anyway.

To her, it wasn’t.

My wife called me over to help, and I walked into the situation not really knowing what had happened.

So I did what I think a lot of fathers do.

I immediately tried to fix it.

I tried to figure out what was wrong, correct the behavior, and get things under control.

But I was frustrated.

And that frustration came out as anger.

I could see it in her.

She wasn’t hearing what I was trying to say anymore.

She was scared.

I walked away, and almost immediately I knew I had gotten it wrong.

Not necessarily what I was trying to teach her.

How I was trying to teach it.

So I went back.

But this time, I approached her differently.

I lowered my voice.

I slowed down.

I listened.

I gave her patience instead of frustration.

And she softened.

The same child.

The same problem.

The same father.

Different approach.

And suddenly we could talk.

That moment stayed with me.

As fathers, we’re told to be strong.

Protect.

Provide.

Lead.

Stand firm.

Push our children when they need pushing.

And sometimes I think we confuse strength with hardness.

We think getting louder makes the message clearer.

We think standing firmer means they’ll listen.

We try to muscle our way through a problem because we’re Dad and we’re supposed to know how to fix it.

But tonight reminded me that my family doesn’t always need the hardened version of me.

The world gets that version.

The version willing to fight.

The version willing to stand between my family and whatever comes for them.

But my children shouldn’t have to feel like they need protection from that version of me too.

They need somewhere safe to land.

They need a father they can fail in front of.

A father they can be angry around.

A father they can tell the truth to.

A father who can correct them without making them question whether they’re still loved.

That doesn’t mean there aren’t boundaries.

It doesn’t mean bad behavior suddenly becomes okay.

And it doesn’t mean being soft means being weak.

I can still stand firm.

I can still say no.

I can still correct my children.

I just don’t have to harden myself to do it.

Before I left her tonight, I hugged her.

Nothing about the problem had magically disappeared.

But something between us had changed.

And maybe that’s what I needed to learn tonight.

There are times when my children will need my strength.

But strength doesn’t always look like a clenched fist and a raised voice.

Sometimes it looks like patience.

Sometimes it looks like understanding.

Sometimes it looks like walking away, realizing you got it wrong, and having enough humility to come back differently.

And sometimes…

strength looks like a hug.
