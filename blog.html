LESSONS WRITTEN IN SCARS

Presence Over Perfection

Fatherhood isn’t about getting everything right. It’s about refusing to stop showing up.

I still remember the moment my wife told me we were going to have a baby.

For a second, everything stood still.

I wish I could tell you my first emotion was excitement—that I immediately pictured teaching my child to ride a bike or cheering from the stands at every game and recital.

It wasn’t.

It was fear.

Not because I didn’t want to be a father, but because I had no idea what being a father actually looked like.

I grew up wondering what it would have been like to have a father who was truly present. Someone who taught me the little things most people take for granted. How to change a tire. How to throw a baseball. How to become a man.

Looking back, I realize I wasn’t really grieving the lessons.

I was grieving the presence.

And suddenly, life was asking me to become the very man I’d spent years wishing I had.

I remember leaving the hospital with my child for the first time, looking around almost expecting someone to stop us.

“You’re really trusting us with this?”

We had no manual. No experience. Just two scared parents trying to figure it out.

That drive home changed everything.

Over the years, I’ve realized that fatherhood was never about perfection.

It’s about presence.

Showing up when you’re exhausted.

Showing up after you’ve lost your temper.

Showing up when shame whispers that your children deserve someone better.

Showing up when you don’t have all the answers but refuse to stop trying.

Because every day you choose to come home, to apologize, to learn, and to love, you’re teaching your children something perfection never could.

I’m still learning.

There are days I feel like I’m getting it right, and there are days I lie awake wondering if I’m giving my children everything they deserve. I don’t think that feeling ever completely disappears.

Maybe that’s because the fathers who worry the most are often the ones who care the deepest.

This isn’t written by a parenting expert.

It’s written by a father who’s still becoming the man his children deserve.

If you’re reading this and you’ve ever felt like you’re falling short, I want you to know something.

You’re not alone.

This isn’t a place where fathers come pretending they’ve figured everything out.

It’s a place where we can be honest.

A place where we can admit we’re tired.

A place where we can talk about the weight we carry, the mistakes we’ve made, and the fear that somehow we aren’t enough.

It’s a place for the fathers trying to break generational cycles.

For the dads carrying wounds from their own childhood.

For the men who keep questioning themselves because they care so deeply about getting it right.

If that’s you, welcome.

Because your children won’t remember every lesson you tried to teach.

They won’t remember every word you said.

They may not even remember every mistake you made.

But I hope they remember one thing.

Dad was there.

Stay.

Dad was there.OBSIDIAN

The Trail Always Teaches

Some lessons can’t be taught. They have to be lived.

Every summer, my daughters and I pack up the car and head somewhere new.

Some people think it’s about the destination.

The mountains.

The lakes.

The cabins.

The pictures we’ll take along the way.

Those things are great, but they’re not the reason we go.

The real reason is the trail.

Every hike gives me something I could never teach sitting around the dinner table.

Before we start, I tell my girls the same thing every time.

“This is life.”

Some parts of the trail are easy.

You’ll laugh.

You’ll run ahead.

You’ll forget you’re even climbing.

Then the trail gets steep.

Your legs burn.

You wonder how much farther there is.

You want to quit.

When that happens, I tell them the same thing every time.

“It’s okay to stop. Catch your breath. Drink some water. Rest if you need to.”

“Just don’t quit.”

A few years ago, we were hiking in New Mexico.

It was six miles round trip, and the last mile and a half felt like it was straight uphill.

By then, everyone was tired.

There were complaints.

A few frustrated tears.

More than once I heard, “Dad? how much farther?”

I smiled and said, “Just keep going. It’ll be worth it.”

I’m not sure they believed me.

Honestly, I’m not sure I would’ve believed me either.

But we kept climbing.

One step.

Then another.

Eventually, we reached the top.

Waiting for us was a quiet mountain lake.

The girls kicked off their shoes and dipped their feet into the cold water.

The complaining disappeared.

The exhaustion disappeared.

Then they looked at me and smiled.

“Dad? you were right.”

“It was worth it.”

As I sat there watching them, I realized I had never really been teaching them about hiking.

I was teaching them about life.

I’m still learning how to be a father.

I don’t have it figured out, and I don’t pretend to.

But if there’s one thing these trips have taught me, it’s this:

Some lessons are better lived than spoken.

For me, that’s the trail.

For you, it may be something completely different.

Whatever it is, find it.

Find the place where your children don’t just hear what you believe—they get to watch you live it.

Because sometimes the greatest lessons a father teaches are the ones he never has to say out loud.

Stay.

Dad was there.OBSIDIAN

LESSONS WRITTEN IN SCARS

Stand Firm

The world will try to shape them. Our job is to shape them first.

One of the hardest parts of being a father is standing firm when the world is constantly trying to pull you away.

Every day there is another voice telling our children what they should believe, who they should become, and what they should value. Some will call the things we teach old-fashioned. Others will say they're outdated or no longer relevant. But truth doesn't become less true just because the world changes its mind.

As fathers, we have to become the pillar our children can lean on when everything around them feels uncertain.

That doesn't mean we stop listening. It doesn't mean we become hard-hearted or unwilling to learn. We should listen. We should ask questions. We should be humble enough to consider what others have to say.

But listening doesn't mean abandoning what we know to be true.

If something doesn't align with the foundation we're trying to build, then it's our responsibility to gently guide our children back—not with fear, not with control, but with love and conviction.

The world will try to shape them.

Our job is to shape them first.

Not into copies of ourselves, but into men and women who know they are loved, who know they are heard, and who have a foundation strong enough to stand when life shakes them.

Because one day they'll leave our home. They'll make their own decisions. They'll wrestle with questions we can't answer for them. They'll discover their own convictions, and they'll have to choose the kind of people they want to become.

We can't walk every road for them.

But we can spend today building a foundation strong enough to support them when those roads become difficult.

And if they stumble?

If life humbles them?

If they lose their way for a season?

I pray they remember the foundation they came from.

Not because their father was perfect, but because he was present.

Because he stood firm.

Because he loved them enough to tell them the truth, even when it wasn't popular.

Because he never stopped believing in them.

I want my daughters to know that no matter how far they wander, they'll always find me standing where I've always been—with my arms open, ready to welcome them home.

Maybe that's one of the greatest callings of a father.

Not to control the path our children choose?

But to build a home, a faith, and a foundation strong enough that they always know where to come back to.

Stay.

Dad was there.OBSIDIAN

LESSONS WRITTEN IN SCARS

Leave Room for the Silence

Sometimes being there means knowing when not to speak.

Yesterday, I took my family to a theme park.

It was hot.

Not regular summer hot. The kind of hot where it feels like you’re sitting inside an oven and everyone starts getting a little irritated just from existing.

We eventually made our way to the water, hoping that would help.

It did.

A little.

But I could tell something was wrong with my oldest daughter.

She didn’t want to be there.

At first, I thought maybe it was the heat. Maybe she was tired. But I know my daughter, and I could tell there was something else going on.

So I did what I think a lot of fathers do.

I tried to fix it.

“What’s wrong?”

“Talk to me.”

“Did something happen?”

“Just tell me what’s going on.”

I wanted her to know I was there. I wanted her to feel heard. I wanted to understand the problem so I could help her solve it.

But the more I tried to pull the answer out of her, the further away she seemed to get.

And I could feel myself getting impatient.

Not angry.

Just desperate for her to let me in.

Eventually, I stopped.

I stopped asking questions.

I stopped trying to fix whatever I didn’t even understand yet.

And I just sat beside her.

For a while, we didn’t say much.

We just shared the same space.

Then, little by little, she started talking.

Not because I found the right question.

Not because I gave her some great piece of fatherly advice.

Because I finally gave her enough room to speak when she was ready.

That hit me.

As fathers, I think a lot of us are wired to fix things.

There’s a problem? Solve it.

Something is broken? Repair it.

Our child is hurting? Make the hurt go away.

But our children aren’t problems to solve.

Sometimes they don’t need an answer.

Sometimes they don’t even need our words.

Sometimes they just need our patience.

And patience is something I’m still learning.

Growing up, I never felt like I had much time to be heard.

If I had something to say, I learned to get it out quickly because I didn’t know how long I had someone’s attention.

My children don’t live that life.

But sometimes that little boy still shows up in me.

The one who thinks everything has to be said right now.

Yesterday reminded me that it doesn’t.

My daughter knew I was there.

She knew she could talk to me.

I didn’t need to force the moment.

I just needed to protect the space for it.

I’m still figuring this fatherhood thing out.

I’m trying to help my children navigate a world that asks them to carry things I wish they didn’t have to think about yet.

And my instinct will probably always be to protect them.

To fix it.

To make it better.

But maybe sometimes protecting them looks different.

Maybe sometimes it’s sitting beside them in the silence.

Not pushing.

Not prying.

Not trying to have the perfect response ready.

Just waiting.

Because sometimes our children don’t need us to give them the answer.

They just need enough time to find the words.

Stay.

Dad was there.OBSIDIAN
